                                           Abstract

This paper presents a Golden Growth Rate (GGR) analysis of the United States for the period
2010 through 2019. A credit counterpart analysis for the period 2009 through 2022 is also
presented with the purpose of analyzing post-financial-crisis monetary growth.

SECTION 1: US Golden Growth Rate (GGR) Analysis1

The GGR is the growth rate of the money supply that is consistent with hitting a defined inflation
target. The deduction of the United States’ GGR uses data from the 2010-2019 period to exclude
the abnormal shocks of the COVID-19 pandemic on economic activities.

Calculation of the GGR features the income form of the Quantity Theory of Money (QTM):
MV=Py, where M stands for the money supply; V stands for the average number of times the
money stock is used in income transactions per unit time; P stands for the price level; y stands
for real GDP. To operationalize the QTM, we can transform the income form into the percent-
change form: ΔM + ΔV = ΔP + Δy.2

One can calculate the GGR by rearranging the Percent-Change Form as: ΔM = ΔP + Δy – ΔV,
where ΔM represents the GGR in broad money supply, M2.3 In this expression, ΔP denotes the
annual inflation target of the Federal Reserve;4, 5 Δy is the average year-over-year (YoY)
percentage change in real GDP in the United States. To obtain change in income velocity ΔV, we

SECTION 2: Framework of Credit Counterpart (CC) Analysis – An Asset Approach7


Credit Counterpart analysis reveals the drivers of changes in broad money supply. The monetary
liabilities constituting M28 have counterparts on the asset side of commercial banks - bank lending,
securities holdings, reserve assets, and residuals adjusting for discrepancies and omitted items.
Expressed in terms of changes, we have:

Liability side (of central banks):
   1. Δ Broad Money = Δ Bank deposits + Δ Cash in circulation with the public, and

Asset side (of commercial banks):
   2. Δ Broad Money = Δ Commercial Bank Lending + Δ Securities (held by the banks) + Δ
        Commercial bank reserves at the central bank +/- Δ Others (net)

The second equation specifies the change in broad money supply as a sum of changes in major
categories of assets held by commercial banks. Our analysis focuses on how market forces and
the Fed’s policies lead to changes in M2 through changes in commercial bank assets over time.
The following section consists of a brief overview of US credit counterparts using annual data
and a detailed analysis of how the Fed’s operations influenced M2 through commercial banks’
lending activities.

For our analysis, we use data from the nine asset accounts in the consolidated balance sheet of
US commercial banks from 2009 through the end of 2022. This is released periodically in H.8
Tables by the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve.9 All data are not seasonally adjusted
and retrieved from the last weekly updates of each year or month from 2009 through 2022.
Figure 3 shows the changes in M2 and major types of commercial bank assets in the United
States from 2009 through 2022, using monthly data.

The credit counterpart analysis breaks down the policies of the Federal Reserve into two major
periods - the pre-pandemic period (2009-2019) and the pandemic period (2020-2022). Each
period is made up of two stages - monetary easing at first and subsequent tightening. Shortly
recapping the policy tools of the Fed is necessary before jumping into analysis.




The following is a summary of the Fed’s monetary policies used in the pre-pandemic period:
  ● Quantitative Easing (QE): QE is the monetary policy tool whereby the central bank
       purchases a large, planned amount of government bonds or other securities to offer
       liquidity in the financial market. In QE1 (November 2008), the Fed initially purchased
       $600 billion of mortgage-backed securities and then ratcheted up the scale and scope of
       the assets that it purchased. In QE2 (November 2010), the Fed aimed to buy $600 billion
       of Treasury securities by the end of June 2011. In QE3 (September 2012), the Federal
       Reserve planned to additionally conduct $40 billion in open-ended purchases of


                                               6
       mortgage-backed securities per month, and the volume of purchase was adjusted
       subsequently with regard to economic conditions.10
     ● Changes in the Fed’s Operating Procedures: To establish the federal funds market as
       the principal target of monetary policies, the Fed started to pay interest on banks’ reserve
       deposits at the Fed in 2008.11 It also created an overnight reverse repurchase agreement
       (ON RRP) facility in 2014.12 Simultaneously, the Fed allowed the federal funds target to
       rise above the zero bound for the first time after 2008.13
     ● Quantitative Tightening (QT): QT is the reverse action of QE. After 2015, the Fed
       aimed to stabilize its balance sheet and formally initiated a QT program in 2018 by
       retiring some of its expired debts. The size of its balance sheet shrank rapidly in 2018
       and 2019, but such pressure on economic activities forced QT to stop after one year.14

During the COVID period, the Fed again pursued expansionary monetary policies, and with a
typical lag, inflation followed. The following are a few policy tools the Fed used to
overstimulate and then cool down the economy from 2020 through 2023:15
    ● QE: The Fed set its rate of purchases to $80 billion per month in Treasuries and $40
        billion in residential and commercial mortgage-backed securities in March 2020. The
        pace of asset purchase remained intact until November 2021. It also purchased
        commercial paper as part of the asset purchase program.
    ● Federal Funds Rate: The Fed lowered the federal funds target to 0-0.25% in March
        2020. However, as inflation hit high points afterwards, the Fed decided to lift the target
        rate seven times consecutively in 2022, pulling it to 4.25%-4.5% at the end of the year.16
    ● Direct Lending Programs: In 2020, the Fed started to offer more direct loans to banks
        by cutting the discount rate from 2.25% to 0.25% to prevent bank panics. It also created
        programs that lent directly to securities firms, state and local governments, and major
        corporate employers.
    ● Reserve Requirement: In March 2020, the FOMC decided to lower the reserve
        requirement of commercial banks to 0%, which was confirmed formally in 2021.
    ● Repo Facilities: The Fed extended the lengths and amount of its repo facilities in the
        federal funds market and set up a permanent Standing Repo Facility to smooth money
        markets in July 2021.

To give an insight into the impacts of the Fed’s operations, we can distinguish the contribution
of changes in each category of bank assets to the changes in M2. Figure 4 on the next page
shows the contribution of bank assets to M2 growth using annual data. For example, in January
2016, where the contribution of reserves was -2.37 percentage points, the contribution of loans
and leases was 5.31 points, and the contribution of residuals was 1.87 percentage points, which
added up to 6.17% - the YoY growth rate in M2.

There are a few general takeaways from Figure 4. One important observation is that
contributions of reserves were significant only after the Fed conducted monetary expansions,
namely in 2010-2011, 2013, and 2020-2021 periods. Another notable observation is that loans
and leases were more important drivers of monetary expansions compared to securities, but
changes in loans and leases were also more volatile than those of securities. Lastly,
contributions of residuals were usually insignificant but moved drastically in 2009, 2013 and
2020 in response to the Fed’s monetary expansions.

We now provide more detail with monthly data. As elaborated before, we can divide the period
of 2009-2022 into three periods: 2009-2015, 2016-2019, 2020-2022. The monthly YoY
contributions of commercial bank assets are shown in Figure 5.18




(1) 2009-2015: As seen in Figure 5, the effects of the three QEs were significant on reserves
    held by commercial banks, resulting in three peaks in their contributions to the growth of
    M2. However, QE1 did not result in the stimulation of bank lending, despite the provision of
    plentiful reserves. Consequently, the Fed came up with QE2, which stabilized the
    contribution of loans and leases in positive ranges in 2011, and QE3 to boost the recovery of
    YoY increases in lending activities. Eventually, the contribution of loans and leases
    recovered in 2012. Simultaneously, the banks were not willing to increase securities
    holdings drastically due to the slow recovery of the economy. The contribution of residuals
    was very positive before QE2 but declined sharply later on. Overall, growth in M2 was
    moderate in all years aside from 2010, with most of the monetary expansion being explained
    by the growth in commercial banks’ reserves, but not credit expansions.

(2) 2016-2019: As economic activities went back to normal levels, the Fed started to stabilize
    the size of its balance sheet after 2015 and retired parts of expired securities after 2018. As a
    result, the contribution of reserves to M2 was mostly negative during the period. Under

   by reducing their lending and securities holdings. As a result of the fact that QT had created
   a liquidity crisis, the Fed quickly abandoned its QT scheme in September 2019, and injected
   $75 billion into the reverse repo market. With insignificant levels of residuals, the M2
   growth rate underwent slight decreases compared to the previous period mainly because of
   slower expansions in lending and negative changes in reserves.



(3) 2020-2022: During the pandemic, the Fed cut the federal funds rate sharply between early
    2020 and mid-2021 and conducted QE. The contribution of reserves surged up to 10% in
    April and May 2020 and remained high throughout most of 2021. Meanwhile, the
    commercial banks refrained from increasing their lending activities drastically as a recession
    occurred. Instead, residuals rose quickly along with securities holdings. All credit
    counterparts, aside from lending, supported the extremely high YoY growth rate in M2 in
    2020 and 2021.

   To counter the high inflation, resulting from excessive monetary growth in 2020-21, the Fed
   started to lift federal funds targets and introduced QT. In consequence, the contribution of
   reserves to the money supply abruptly fell in just a few months. The contribution of
   securities declined, and the contribution of lending increased. The contribution of residuals
   also plummeted almost instantly after the Fed put forth the policies. Despite the acceleration
   in lending, the overall result of sharp decreases in the contribution margins of reserves,
   securities, and residuals was a plummeting rate of YoY growth in M2 from above 10% in
   December 2021 to negative levels in December 2022.
